A breach bulletin changed its mind, and a new model arrived with a retention clause attached. Both come down to what you hand to someone else's platform and for how long.

## 🔥 Fireworks AI says the breach reached customer datasets and model weights
In October Fireworks said attackers had captured secrets stored for evaluation jobs, that it had found no evidence of dataset or model access so far, and that its review was ongoing. Its update of 9 November says account and billing information was affected too, and for a limited set of accounts so were secrets, datasets and incomplete models. Fireworks links it to a financially motivated group that sells stolen LLM access, and says a third-party cloud provider hosting its R&D machines was hit on 23 October and used on the 24th.

A bulletin's "no evidence of" is a snapshot, not a verdict. If you fine-tune on a hosted platform, find out where your datasets sit between jobs and whose machines can touch them. Fireworks sees no impact on Inference, Training SDK or Nexus, which are zero data retention by default.

**Affected:** customers Fireworks notifies; a limited set of accounts had secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights accessed · **Fixed in:** not applicable (Fireworks lists hardening steps and has disabled self-serve dataset uploads and downloads for Batch Inference and dataset-based training) · **Severity:** not stated in the bulletin · **Stopgap:** the October bulletin says to create new API keys for revoked ones, rotate third-party credentials, and review access logs · **If compromised:** reply to your incident notification email

[Source](https://fireworks.ai/security/bulletins/october-2026-security-incident-update) · [October bulletin](https://fireworks.ai/security/bulletins/october-2026-security-incident)

## 🚀 Claude Fable 5.2 is out, and Mythos 5.2 is the same model for vetted users
Anthropic says Fable and Mythos 5.2 are the same underlying model. Fable keeps cybersecurity and biology safeguards that Mythos lacks and is open to everyone; Mythos goes to vetted users through Anthropic's verification programs. Fable costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, and Artificial Analysis gives it 65 on its intelligence index, first of the 74 models it lists.

Anthropic's own numbers put it at 69.9% on FrontierSWE v2 against 62.3% for Opus 5.5, yet Anthropic still recommends Opus 5.5 as the daily driver for most coding and knowledge tasks. The catch is in the terms: AWS says Fable 5.2 is a covered model, so by default its use requires retention for up to 30 days and human safety review. Check that against your data classification before you move traffic.

**Try this:** replay your 20 hardest agent tasks on Fable 5.2 and on Opus 5.5, and compare cost per solved task.

[Source](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) · [AWS blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/introducing-claude-fable-5-2-on-aws/) · [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-fable-5-2)

## 🤖 Kong's Token Vault stops agents from ever holding a user's upstream tokens
Announced on 4 November, Token Vault adds per-user OAuth to Kong AI Gateway, so an agent can call GitHub, Slack or Atlassian through MCP servers and APIs on behalf of a signed-in user. Kong says agents never receive the upstream access tokens, which are encrypted with a key only the gateway data planes hold, taken from your own secrets manager. Kong says it's available today in the 2.3 release, included with all Kong Gateway subscriptions.

The design moves token refresh, storage and revocation out of agent code and out of the prompt, where a prompt-injected agent could be talked into revealing it. The trade-off is that the gateway becomes the single place a credential can be lost, so size its availability and blast radius like an identity provider. The security claims are Kong's own.

[Source](https://konghq.com/blog/product-releases/kong-token-vault)

## 🔬 MemoryDojo gives memory poisoning a benchmark
A preprint submitted on 6 November builds 155 multi-session trajectories across four domains and tests five memory systems, plus a memory-aware way to optimise the poison. The abstract says all of the systems are susceptible.

If your agent keeps memory between sessions, that store is a write path into future prompts, so it deserves the trust boundary you give any input: validate writes, keep provenance, expire entries. Read the paper's setup before quoting its results.

[Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09721)

## ✍️ Simon Willison hides agent-written PR descriptions behind a details tag
Most changes to Simon's projects now arrive as PRs opened by Claude Code or Codex, and the descriptions those agents write are accurate and comprehensive, which is also the problem: they overwhelm. His fix is a `<details><summary>` block, with the model name and often the effort level as the summary, under a short opener he writes himself. His promise to the reader is that the opener represents his human judgement.

It's a cheap review contract: the human owns the claim, the agent owns the detail. Would your team's PR template survive that split?

[Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/9/details-summary/)

## 🏗️ Google's Kubernetes AI stack leans on cache-aware routing
Google Cloud's 10 November map of an open AI stack on Kubernetes features llm-d, which reached 1.0 on 20 October per Red Hat. Google cites prefix-cache-aware routing lifting the KV cache hit rate from 35% to 70% and cutting median time-to-first-token by 70–85% on multi-turn agent workloads. On InfoQ, Red Hat's Edoardo Vacchi says plain Kubernetes load balancing suits LLMs poorly, because a conversation does better staying on one vLLM instance, and vLLM doesn't do automatic flow control, which llm-d adds.

If your agents hold long conversations, round-robin is a cost bug. The trade-off is affinity against balance: sticky routing can pile load onto one replica, and the numbers are Google's and Red Hat's, not an independent test.

**Try this:** replay one day of agent traffic through round-robin and through prefix-aware routing, and compare hit rate and median time-to-first-token.
