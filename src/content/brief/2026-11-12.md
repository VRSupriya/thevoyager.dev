Three of today's stories come down to one question: who gets to open the door when an agent builds or buys something for you?

## 🔥 Grok Build apps were open to anyone with the link
Security firm Claroc, in a post dated 10 November, tried 43,551 Grok app addresses. Of the 37,571 that were online, 35,578 opened with no sign-in. In the paid account it tested, the publish panel started on "Anyone with the link".

It found a new-hire onboarding plan from SpaceXSI (the company behind Grok), a hospital group's staff roster of more than 3,500 people, and an ICU patient's labs. SpaceXSI says apps are private until their creator shares them, and removed the 24 apps built by its own and X staff.

If your teams publish AI-built apps, the lesson is boring and useful: audit the sharing default, not just the code.

**Affected:** apps published from Grok Build to grok.me subdomains · **Fixed in:** no fix stated; SpaceXSI says apps are private until their creator chooses to share · **Severity:** no CVE or rating stated; Claroc estimates about 2% of open apps held material it would rate high or critical · **Stopgap:** Claroc advises "Invited users" or "Only you" for internal apps, checking each app's access setting, testing sign-in from outside the company and reviewing scripts for exposed credentials · **If compromised:** Claroc says it is notifying affected organisations directly
[Source](https://blog.claroc.io/ghostshares-grok-build-apps/)

## 🚀 A new open-weights coder that trades accuracy for speed
NaiveAI's Naive-N0.5-Flash, reviewed by Latent Space on 10 November, peaked at about 2,100 tokens a second on one 8-GPU Blackwell node. The review itself calls that "an upper bound, not a typical rate". The model is open-weights and builds on Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.5. NaiveAI's target price is $0.10 in, $0.40 out per million tokens.

The benchmarks are NaiveAI's own and mixed: 71.9 on NL2Repo against 68.2 for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, but 67.8 on DeepSWE v1.1 against 77.0. The reviewer measured median response times of 3.1 seconds against 5.3 (GLM) and 10.1 (DeepSeek), on 10 questions per difficulty level with accuracy not graded.

Fast and slightly weaker can beat slow and better when an agent loops dozens of times.

**Try this:** run 20 tasks from your own repo through your current coder and through this one, and compare wall-clock time per accepted patch, not benchmark scores.
[Source](https://latent.space/p/naiveai/)

## 🤖 United tells third-party AI agents to stay out
United announced on 11 November that it will "prohibit use by unauthorized, third-party personal AI agents or assistants", CBS reports. United's reasons, as CBS quotes them: sharing credentials with such assistants "can expose sensitive information", and their actions "may result in unintended purchases, account changes". Engadget adds a quote from CEO Scott Kirby: "I don't want to create another middleman."

If you're building agents that act on a user's behalf, plan for sites that won't accept a borrowed login. My read is that an official API or delegated access is the route that survives, though neither article mentions one. United's own statement wasn't available to read, so this rests on the two outlets.
[Source](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/united-airlines-bans-ai-agents-booking-flights/) · [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2314121/united-is-restricting-ai-agents-from-booking-flights/)

## 🔬 ADIndex makes DeepSeek-style sparse attention cheaper to run
In DeepSeek Sparse Attention, the step that picks which tokens to attend to can take over half of prefill GPU time at long context. A preprint submitted on 7 November, ADIndex, trains lighter, layer-adaptive indexers to shrink that step.

The authors report kernel speedups of up to 6.22x for prefill and 3.40x for decoding at 256K context, and 1.54x end-to-end serving at 128K, with near-lossless results on LongBench, LongBench v2 and RULER. They tested DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5.1, implemented in SGLang on NVIDIA B300 GPUs. The paper doesn't link code.

If you serve long contexts on DeepSeek-style models, watch for a release: the gain sits in a stage most teams never profile.
[Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12956) · [The paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12956)

## ✍️ Workday's agent platform, in numbers that are small and honest
LangChain's write-up of Workday's internal agent platform (10 November) says the team started at 4 people and is now roughly 30. A team can now get a prototype running in staging "in under a day", where it used to take 4 to 5 months.

The architecture is LangGraph for orchestration, LangSmith for tracing and evals, a Workday SDK that routes through an internal LLM gateway, and authentication and security requirements built into the platform. The post is a customer story and doesn't say how many agents are in production.

The transferable bit is the shape: a small platform team that owns the paved road, so product teams don't each rebuild auth.
[Source](https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-workday-built-an-enterprise-scale-ai-agent-platform-with-langgraph-and-langsmith)

## 🏗️ Codex on Bedrock: pick the rollout path before the pilot
AWS's guide (11 November) lays out three ways to run OpenAI's Codex against Bedrock: native AWS access through IAM Identity Center, AgentCore Gateway, or an LLM gateway you already run. Its recommended default is "Start with Native AWS Access for a small, least-privilege pilot."

The choice is really about where you want controls to live: IAM and CloudWatch telemetry, Bedrock Guardrails at a managed gateway, or request-path limits in your own gateway. Hard budgets and rate limits need gateway policy. Codex itself still runs on the developer's workstation in every path.
[Source](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/openai-codex-on-amazon-bedrock/)
