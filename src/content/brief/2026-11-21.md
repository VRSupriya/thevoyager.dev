Somebody mailed two million prompts into the AI supply chain and then watched who opened them. The answers are less comfortable than you'd hope.

## 🔥 Canary prompts show AI providers leaking customer requests
HD Moore at Dragos sent about two million prompts with unique URLs and canary credentials to widely used AI services over five months. Data from at least three providers leaked, and by the post's account none knew until Dragos told them. The biggest case: a couple of hundred AWS keys, some from requests rejected before any model ran.

The design lesson is that a privacy flag only binds the first hop. Through a router or reseller you inherit every downstream logging choice, and agents send whatever credentials they can read.

**Affected:** three AI providers, not named in the post · **Fixed in:** not applicable; Dragos says it is working with about a dozen providers, one has published a preliminary security notice and another has not disclosed · **Severity:** not stated in the post · **Stopgap:** go directly to a first-tier provider, choose upstream providers yourself, stay away from reseller marketplaces, self-host if you can, enable secret filtering (not a panacea), add the user agents `artex-agent` and `artex-judge-r121` to detections · **If compromised:** not stated in the post as a procedure

**Try this:** put a free canary token in one agent's context, keep your current gateway as the baseline, and count fetches over a week. Any hit is an incident.

[Source](https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens)

## 🚀 Inkling 2 Mini: a 26B open model that fits on a laptop
Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini on 20 November with open weights under Apache 2.0. It has 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B active, and a 1M-token context, and it takes text and images in. The model card also notes 5.4B effective active parameters per token because it loops through some layers three times.

The company's own numbers include 79.2% on SWE-bench Verified and 71.6% on Terminal Bench v2.1. Ollama runs it on Macs with at least 32GB of RAM at launch, with GGUF builds via Unsloth and llama.cpp. For a local coding model that's a real option, but those are the maker's benchmarks, and no Hugging Face Inference Provider hosts it yet, though Thinking Machines offers it on its own Tinker platform.

**Try this:** run your usual repo-fix task set against it locally, with your current hosted model as the baseline, and compare pass rate and cost per solved task.

[Source](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) · [Model card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)

## 🤖 Tune the model to the harness, not the other way round
Hugging Face trained LoRA adapters for Qwen3.8-27B, one per agent harness (Pi, Hermes Agent, OpenCode), using RL inside each harness as shipped. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, Pi went from a 39% to a 55% pass rate while average tokens per task fell from about 2.9K to 1.5K.

For you, the lesson is that harness and model are coupled more tightly than most evals assume. A merged "super-adapter" lifted mean pass@1 from 39.5% to 49.0% across six pairs, but out-of-distribution results on AutomationBench were mostly within noise, and the authors flag reasoning leakage before tool calls. Worth a look if you run a fixed harness on an open model.

[Source](https://huggingface.co/blog/harness-adapters-at-scale) · [Adapters](https://huggingface.co/collections/harness-hub/qwen38-27b-harness-adapters)

## 🔬 Agent memory that is just a grep-able log
A preprint, "Forget Freely, Grep Later", keeps the agent's full history as a plain-text log and lets the model search it with regex. There's no embedding, index or extraction step. With a Qwen3.5-9B trained by RL, the authors report 95–97% on ContextBench against at most 21% for lossy baselines.

If it holds up, it removes a whole component from agent memory designs and trades it for tokens spent on search. Those are research results from one small model, so treat it as a design to test, not a replacement for your memory store.

[Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25123) · [Paper HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25123v1) · [Code](https://github.com/limenlp/GrepMem)

## ✍️ When free trials become someone else's inference budget
Simon Willison flagged a video by Claire Vo of ChatPRD about a flood of "token fraud": people looping free trials and promo codes, then tunnelling through her agent to use the model underneath. Some resell the tokens; others run commercial work on top. She's ending free trials entirely, and Willison agrees.

If your product puts an agent behind a signup form, the trial is a metered API with a login page. Per-account caps and abuse signals belong in the design before launch, not after the first bill.

[Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/20/token-fraud-is-here-and-its-bad/)

## 🏗️ Signal tells OS-level AI agents to keep out
Signal says it's restricting the AI agents built into mobile operating systems. On iOS 27 it blocks screenshots in the app, on Android it turns on "Screen security" when it detects an assistant reading the screen, and on macOS it can only ask users to exclude Signal in System Settings. Signal calls its own remedy incomplete, because agents can still reach the filesystem.

Anyone building an agent that reads the screen or uses accessibility permissions should expect apps to push back, and plan for a path that doesn't depend on seeing everything. Signal is asking Apple, Google and Microsoft for a standard opt-out.

[Source](https://signal.org/blog/protecting-your-privacy/)
