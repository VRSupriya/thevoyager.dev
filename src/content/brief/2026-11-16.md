Both lead stories today are about agents holding credentials: one in an attacker's hands, one under Uber's rules.

## 🔥 The FBI says an open-source AI hacking agent is in real intrusions
An FBI FLASH of 13 November describes ARTEX, an open-source, LLM-driven agent platform attackers have used since at least August 2026 to automate intrusions from reconnaissance to data theft. It brings no model of its own: it plugs into Claude, GPT, DeepSeek, GLM, Grok or Kimi, and the FBI says DeepSeek Flash models dominate what it saw. Its developer pulled the repository on 8 October, but forks and a commercial ARTEX PRO live on.

```stats
3 hours | from first targeting to privileged access in some intrusions (FBI)
1,600+ | publicly exposed ARTEX consoles found by researchers by early November
```

The FBI says the agents do best against overlooked systems: partner portals, mobile backends, legacy services. If your inventory of those is thin, assume it's the first place a fast, tireless scanner finds you, and put admin interfaces behind private paths.

**Affected:** organisations with internet-facing apps, self-service portals, cloud infrastructure and partner or supplier systems, across sectors from finance and government to healthcare and energy; the FLASH names no vulnerable product versions, and cites a ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise that reached more than 2,250 downstream tenants · **Fixed in:** the FLASH lists no patch or fixed version, since ARTEX is a tool, not a vulnerability; the developer removed the repository on 8 October, but forks continue · **Severity:** not stated in the FLASH · **Stopgap:** restrict cloud admin, Kudu/SCM and other management interfaces to trusted networks, rate-limit sensitive APIs, scan pipelines for embedded secrets · **If compromised:** preserve sign-in and audit records first; if suspicious activity is confirmed, revoke unnecessary permissions and rotate credentials, keep affected ConnectWise application connections disabled until ConnectWise gives restoration instructions, and report to your local FBI field office or ic3.gov

[Source](https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2026/261113.pdf) · [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fbi-warns-artex-ai-hacking-tool-is-powering-global-cyberattacks/)

## 🤖 Uber governs its agents in three layers
Uber's engineering post of 6 November describes how it keeps autonomous agents inside bounds across their lifecycle: validation when an agent is registered, enforcement while it runs, and continuous assurance afterwards. The pieces are named: an AI Registry, an analysis step called Argus, AI Guards, runtime hooks called Agent Guard, and LangFx. Controls include personas, scoped intents, constraints and provenance, and the assurance layer keeps a unified execution trace that can replay interactions.

For architects, the useful idea is that an agent is a registered artefact with an identity and declared intent, so policy can be checked before it runs and again while it does. It's a design description, not a results report: the post gives no block rates or latency figures.

[Source](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/securing-ai-agents/) · [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/11/uber-secure-ai-framework/)

## 🔬 XYBench asks whether your assistant notices the wrong question
Ai2's XYBench, described in a Hugging Face post of 13 November, tests whether an assistant spots the misconception behind a request instead of answering it literally. It has 8,115 questions drawn from Stack Overflow, Stack Exchange, manual curation and WikiHow guides, and two human reviewers checked 500 model responses to validate the automated judge. Code and the dataset are released, and the post says it won the Best Resource Paper award at EMNLP 2026.

The point for builders is that coding assistants fail quietly here: they do the thing asked, not the thing needed. Worth a look before you trust a judge-model score on your own assistant.

**Try this:** pull 20 tickets from your tracker where the stated fix was the wrong one. Run them through your assistant and count how often it names the underlying goal before answering.

[Source](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/xybench) · [Code](https://github.com/allenai/XYBench)

## ✍️ Sebastian Raschka on how LLMs know where a token sits
In a post of 15 November, Raschka walks from GPT-2's learned position vectors to RoPE and the variants in recent open-weight models. One number lands well: GPT-2 Medium's position table is 0.30% of its weights at 1,024 positions, but at a 100k context it "would make up >22%" of them. He also notes MiniMax-M3 rotates 64 of 128 head dimensions, and SmolLM3 skips RoPE in one of every four layers. PyTorch snippets are included.

If you compare long-context models or tune serving settings, this explains why extending context is a design choice with costs, not a flag. It's a tutorial rather than news, so read it when you have twenty quiet minutes.

[Source](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-positional-embeddings)

## 🏗️ Google puts agent conversation logs in BigQuery
A Google Cloud post of 14 November says conversation logging in BigQuery, the Conversational Analytics API (with VPC-SC, CMEK and data residency), A2A access to BigQuery data agents in Gemini Enterprise, and agent monitoring dashboards are generally available. Native BigQuery evaluations and per-project, per-user and per-agent daily token quotas are in preview. Logs record input, output, thought, cached and total tokens per turn, plus latency.

Putting traces in the warehouse means the access rules you already have apply to them, but it ties your agent telemetry to one cloud. The post mentions only "promotional trial pricing" with no end date.

**Try this:** if you run agents on Google's stack, enable logging for one agent and compute tokens per resolved conversation from the log table. Compare it with the cost figure your dashboard shows today.

[Source](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/enterprise-ai-agent-observability-with-bigquery-and-looker/)
