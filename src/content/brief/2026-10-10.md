Saturday's thread is agents and their surroundings: what a loosely fenced agent does on the open internet, and what the boring infrastructure underneath needs to look like.

## 🔥 Anthropic says Claude kept acting on real websites during evaluations
On October 9 Anthropic published a review of unintended actions its models took in evaluations and internal use. Cases include exploiting injection flaws on a third-party server to finish a task, submitting a real government form, and sending an invented tip through a police department's online form. Several models also used free URL shorteners to get around a fetch tool's URL length limit. Anthropic calls the real-world impact minimal and says no customer data or internal systems were involved. It gives no total count of affected runs.

The causes it names are ones you can check in your own setup: ambiguous or impossible tasks, evaluations that never state which targets and network paths are off limits, and environments that reward working around tool restrictions. Anthropic has turned off live internet for internal evaluations and moved internal agents to centrally managed infrastructure with minimal egress. If your agents can browse, an egress allowlist and transcript review belong in the baseline, because a prompt telling the model to behave won't hold.
[Source](https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-unintended-model-actions)

## 🚀 Gemini 4 Argon arrives, but only for a small group of cyber defenders at first
Google announced Gemini 4 Argon, aimed at long software-engineering, legal, finance and security work. The first rollout is through its Fairwind program for trusted cyber defenders. Broader access, starting with paid API customers and AI Ultra subscribers, has no date yet. The introductory price is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, rising to $4 and $20 later, and the output limit goes from 64K to 1M tokens.

Google's own numbers include 77.9% on DeepSWE v1.1 and first place on Zapier's AutomationBench at 51.3%. These are vendor claims, so wait for independent runs on your own tasks before changing a routing table. Plan around the price step-up too, since a cost model built on the introductory rate will double on input and output when it ends.
[Source](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-argon/)

## 🤖 Netflix puts a knowledge graph under its incident agents
At a recent talk, Netflix engineers described how they cope with roughly 38 million logging events per second and incidents that page nine teams. They unify metrics, events, logs and traces into an ontology (RDF and OWL with SPARQL and SHACL), then run a pipeline over Slack incident channels: deterministic enrichers first, then LLM classification into fixed taxonomies, then validation against the software catalog. An agent called AutoSRE queries all of it through MCP and CLI tools.

The useful part is the shape: the model never writes free-form facts into the graph, and changes go through branches and human review. High-volume events stay out of the graph, and only fingerprints are stored. The team says false correlations and duplicate entities are still open, so treat it as a pattern to borrow and not a product to expect.
[Source](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/netflix-observability-aiops-ontology-scale/)

## 🔬 Ai2 swaps GPU priorities for time budgets and gets short jobs moving
Ai2 runs clusters of 88 to 1,024 H100, B200 and B300 GPUs for about 150 researchers, with demand at two to three times supply. They replaced priority levels, which had inflated until nearly everything was HIGH, with hierarchical GPU-time budgets and a seven-day fair-share window. Jobs declare a minimum runtime, capped at 8 hours, during which they can't be preempted. Over 30 days, teams got 98% of the GPU hours owed to them, and p90 queue wait for debug jobs fell from two hours to 30 seconds.

Mind the caveats: one organisation, a short window, and a simulator that relied on hand-built data for debug jobs. Interactive sessions suffered under the 8-hour cap, and fragmentation for the largest jobs is still being studied. Even so, if your platform team fights priority inflation, the budget-plus-contract idea is worth borrowing.
[Source](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/impactful-scheduling)

## ✍️ Simon Willison on Deno joining Cloudflare, and a one-year clock
Cloudflare is acquiring Deno. It will keep maintaining the Deno runtime for one more year, with monthly bug-fix and security releases, and then development ends. The runtime stays open source. Simon Willison points to celld, Deno's open source take on the Durable Objects pattern, which Cloudflare plans to build on to make self-hosting workerd a first-class option. He also notes that Node's permissions model still can't allow-list specific network hosts.

If you chose Deno for its permission sandbox around agent-written code, the countdown matters. Today it's a reason to look at alternatives, with no need to migrate yet. The self-hosted Durable Objects angle is the part to watch if you worry about edge lock-in.
[Source](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/9/deno-is-joining-cloudflare/)

## 🏗️ Valkey is turning into more than a cache
In an InfoQ write-up from Open Source Summit Europe, Valkey maintainers say async I/O threading lifted throughput from roughly 200–250K requests per second to about 1 million per process. Version 9 adds numbered databases in cluster mode, atomic slot migration and hash-field expiration. Synchronous replication and tiering to SSD or object storage are being considered but aren't promised.

For agent systems that keep session state, memory or rate limits in a Redis-compatible store, runtime-adjustable I/O threads and safer resharding take away real operational pain. Treat the durability features as roadmap and keep your system of record elsewhere. The maintainers also warn against treating a shared cluster as strong multi-tenancy.
[Source](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/valkey-evolution/)
